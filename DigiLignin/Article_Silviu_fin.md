## Abstract

Lignin-based polyurethanes (PUs) offer a compelling route towards sustainable material development, yet the challenge of designing chemical formulations with targeted properties, such as glass transition temperature (Tg), remains unresolved. In this work, we present a systematic approach to explore key structural parameters—including lignin content, polyol chain length, isocyanate functionality, and mixing ratios—across 136 unique formulations synthesised over two years of laboratory work. By harnessing this small dataset, we develop a stacking ensemble model that combines gradient boosting, random forest, support vector regression, LASSO, and elastic net base learners with a Ridge meta-model. Rigorous validation using proper train/validation/test splitting with out-of-fold meta-feature generation and hyperparameter tuning yields MAE = 11.31°C (R² = 0.687, 95% CI: 10.36–12.26°C) with a near-zero generalisability gap (0.02°C), confirming genuine predictive capability without data leakage. The stacking ensemble outperforms all individual base models by up to 30%, effectively mitigating the severe overfitting exhibited by tree-based learners. A two-stage cascade model enables pre-synthesis prediction (MAE = 16.67°C) without requiring post-synthesis characterisation data. Variance inflation factor analysis quantifies the inherent multicollinearity of compositional features and demonstrates its negligible impact on ensemble prediction accuracy. Structure-property interpretation through the lens of crosslink density, free volume theory, and hydrogen bonding provides mechanistic insights and actionable formulation design guidelines for targeting specific Tg ranges. An adaptive grid search algorithm combined with parallel coordinates visualisation translates model predictions into a user-friendly interface for rapid formulation optimisation. This study establishes a framework for ML-assisted sustainable polymer design that addresses the practical challenges of small datasets in materials informatics.

## Introduction

The global polyurethane (PU) industry is experiencing growing pressure to develop environmentally friendly and bio-based alternatives in response to ecological concerns. This shift is driven by the increasing demand for high-performance materials in sectors like construction, automotive, furniture, and footwear, where sustainability has become a key priority.

Lignin, the second most abundant biopolymer on Earth after cellulose, represents a largely untapped resource for sustainable materials development. The global paper and pulp industry generates approximately 50 million tons of technical lignin annually, with the majority currently burned for energy recovery or discarded as waste [REF]. This underutilization represents both an environmental challenge and an economic opportunity. Valorizing lignin into high-value polyurethane materials aligns with circular economy principles and reduces dependence on petroleum-derived polyols. However, lignin's inherent heterogeneity—arising from variations in botanical source, extraction method, and processing conditions—creates significant challenges for predictable material design. Unlike synthetic polyols with well-defined molecular weights and functionalities, lignin exhibits batch-to-batch variability in hydroxyl content, molecular weight distribution, and aromatic structure. This variability makes traditional trial-and-error formulation development inefficient and resource-intensive.

The glass transition temperature (Tg) is a critical property that determines the usable temperature range and application suitability of polyurethane materials. For lignin-based polyurethanes, achieving target Tg values is particularly challenging due to the complex, non-linear relationships between formulation parameters (lignin content, isocyanate ratio, co-polyol type) and final properties. Conventional approaches rely on extensive experimental campaigns to map the formulation-property landscape, which is time-consuming and costly. Machine learning offers a transformative alternative by learning these complex relationships from existing data, enabling rapid prediction of Tg for untested formulations and accelerating the development cycle. By reducing the experimental burden, ML-driven approaches can make lignin-based polyurethanes more economically competitive with petroleum-based alternatives, thereby facilitating their commercial adoption.

Historically, the creation of new materials has been a laborious process, heavily reliant on extensive laboratory experimentation. While crucial for material innovation, this conventional approach often proves inefficient, lengthy, and expensive. A prime example is the challenge of regulating thermomechanical properties in PUs, which necessitates multiple experiments to adjust ingredient proportions, catalysts, and production methods, potentially hindering the rapid commercialization of new materials.

Recent progress in data-centric methodologies, however, offers a promising avenue for expediting PU discovery. The field of materials science has seen a surge in the application of artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) techniques. Researchers are now employing sophisticated algorithms to forecast polymer structures and properties with remarkable precision, thereby reducing the need for costly physical experiments. These models achieve this by automating the prediction of critical performance metrics such as the glass transition property (Tg), even when working with sparse datasets.

Despite these advancements, not all ML methods can deal effectively with small experimental datasets. One of the primary limitations of traditional ML algorithms is their tendency to overfit when data is limited, meaning the model captures noise rather than underlying patterns. This issue becomes particularly significant when developing advanced materials like PUs, where small deviations in formulation can lead to large variations in performance metrics. Furthermore, certain models struggle with capturing non-linear relationships that are often present in experimental datasets, requiring more sophisticated approaches to make accurate predictions from limited data.

To overcome these limitations, ensemble methods have emerged as powerful tools. These ML techniques combine multiple models, known as base learners, to improve prediction accuracy and robustness. Random Forests (RF), for instance, construct multiple decision trees and aggregate their predictions. Tao et al., evaluated 79 different ML models to predict the Tg of polymers, using various polymer representations and feature engineering approaches. RF models were among the top performers, particularly when using a polymer's repeat unit as the structural representation. Ensemble models using polynomial base models have also been shown to improve prediction accuracy, moreover, in contrast to RF-based models, the final predictive model can be reformulated as a single model instance significantly improving computational efficiency at production level. Such models were successfully used for designing hyperbranched polymers as dispersing agents in inks. Gradient Boosting Regression (GBR), another powerful ensemble method, has also proven effective in materials informatics. Unlike RF, which builds trees in parallel, GBR constructs trees sequentially, with each new tree correcting the errors of the previous ones. Albuquerque et al. used GBR to analyze various formulations of biobased epoxy resins. However, the limited dataset in that study (35 samples) resulted in significant overfitting, highlighting the need for more robust approaches to handle small data scenarios.

In addressing the challenges, this study proposes an innovative approach that combines domain expertise with ensemble ML techniques for optimizing sustainable, lignin-based PUs. Traditional methods such as cross-validation and hyperparameter tuning are effective for refining individual models, but ensemble approaches—like stacking and bagging—offer the advantage of improving overall performance by leveraging multiple algorithms. Stacking, for instance, uses a meta-model to combine the predictions of various base models, which has been shown to significantly enhance predictive accuracy. Esmaeili et al. applied this technique to improve the prediction of polymer solubility parameters by integrating Support Vector Machines (SVMs), Neural Networks (NNs), and RFs, with a linear regressor serving as the meta-model. Similarly, bagging, which trains models on random subsets of the data to reduce variance, has proven effective for managing heterogeneous datasets. The utility of bagging was demonstrated for improving the prediction of polymer mechanical properties and the robustness of polymer degradation models, respectively.

This study builds on these advancements by combining stacking and bagging techniques with domain-specific knowledge to enhance the predictive accuracy and formulation optimization of lignin-based PUs. A range of models - linear (e.g., Lasso, ElasticNet), kernel-based (e.g., SVMs), and tree-based (e.g., RF, GBR) - are employed to capture both linear and non-linear relationships within the data. Linear models, in particular, have shown consistent performance in polymer research. By integrating the strengths of these diverse models, the proposed approach seeks to establish more reliable data driven structure-property relationships and improve the prediction accuracy of sustainable PU formulations, particularly when working with small experimental datasets. A user-friendly interface that incorporates the model as its core is introduced, enabling chemists to visualize recipe formulations based on desired properties, exemplified by the Tg. This interface simplifies the interaction with the model, removing the need for chemists to have in-depth knowledge of ML techniques and allowing them to focus on leveraging its capabilities with ease.

The central research question guiding this study is: Can ML models, in combination with domain expertise, effectively predict and optimize the formulations of sustainable, lignin-based PUs, especially in the context of limited data? Specifically, we investigate: (1) which formulation and characterization parameters most strongly influence Tg, (2) how ensemble methods can mitigate the overfitting tendencies of individual learners on small datasets, (3) how to disentangle the roles of formulation parameters and post-synthesis characterization measurements (e.g., swelling ratio) in prediction, and (4) how machine learning predictions can be interpreted through the lens of polymer physics to provide mechanistic insights and formulation design guidelines.

### Machine Learning

#### Hardware and Software

All calculations were performed on a HP laptop with a 13th Gen Intel® Core™ i7-1365U processor running at 1.80GHz. Python v. 3.10 was used as programming language.

#### Dataset Preparation and Preprocessing

The experimental raw data (dataset.csv in Supporting Information), initially compiled in excel spreadsheet format, was converted to a CSV file and imported into a Python environment for preprocessing. The preprocessing consisted of several steps: categorical feature encoding using LabelEncoder to transform nominal variables into numerical representations, dropping instances with missing values and feature scaling using RobustScaler to normalize the dataset comprising both numeric and categorical features. We chose RobustScaler for its resilience to outliers and suitability for heterogeneous data types.

The preprocessed dataset comprised nine input features and one target variable (Tg). The first eight features represented experimental parameters used in the synthesis process. The ninth feature (swelling ratio) and the target variable (Tg) were both characterization values obtained from the synthesized product (see Table 1). The raw dataset contained instances with no values in the target (e.g., "NaN" for Tg) as well as instances with 0 wt% lignin content. We removed these rows, which reduced the dataset from 180 instances to 136.

#### Feature Selection

We applied two methods to identify the features most critical for developing the ML model.

The first method was Principal Component Analysis (PCA). PCA is a statistical technique used in feature selection through reduction of the number of variables. It works by transforming the original features of a dataset into a new set of uncorrelated variables called principal components (PCs). These components are linear combinations of the original features and are ordered so that the first few capture the most significant variance in the data. In order to determine the most important features, we aimed to identify those that explain more than 90 % of the total variance. This threshold provides an effective balance between retaining the majority of the data's variability and reducing the dataset's dimensionality. We computed the loading for each feature, which represents the contribution of that feature in forming each PC. Higher loading indicates a stronger influence on the corresponding component.

We ranked the features based on two key metrics: the average absolute loading and the maximum absolute loading. The average absolute loading is the mean of the absolute values of a feature's loading across the selected PCs. This reflects its overall importance across all considered components. The maximum absolute loading, on the other hand, identifies the highest absolute value of a feature's loading across the PCs. This highlights its strongest contribution to any single component.

The second approach employed a wrapper method to identify the most effective combination of features for predictive performance. This method systematically evaluated different feature subsets in conjunction with model training and optimization. We defined two feature lists: "mandatory" features always included in the model and "optional" features explored in every possible combination (see Table 1 for feature designation). The wrapper method generated all subsets of optional features and combined them with the mandatory features, creating diverse feature combinations. For each combination, we trained the model using the ensemble method detailed in Figure 2 to predict the Tg. These results are presented in section 3.2. By iterating through all possible features and model combinations and evaluating their performance based on predefined metrics, the wrapper method identified the optimal combination that yielded the best predictive accuracy. This approach ensured that feature selection was directly tied to the model's performance. Notably, during the early stages of the study, we observed low performing models when using only the four input variables (e.g., the parameters shown in Figure 1). This prompted us to integrate additional features, which included the swelling ratio output as a input feature. The results presented in Supporting Information section 3.2 demonstrate how datasets without swelling ratio return a higher MAE and larger standard deviation than when swelling ratio is used.

After completing the PCA and wrapper analyses, we assessed the results and we compared the outcomes against domain expertise and a correlation analysis of features. The selection of the dataset features was ultimately based on the model performance with the lowest Mean Absolute Error (MAE) values. This integration guided our selection of the optimal feature set for the final ML model, balancing predictive power with interpretability for Tg prediction. Additional results of the PCA and wrapper analysis are available in Supporting Information section 3.

#### Ensemble Learning Regression

The ensemble method employed in this study combined multiple base models with a meta-model to improve predictive performance. The implementation scheme is shown in Figure 2 and it is commonly referred to as stacking ensemble.

**Figure 2** Process description for developing the stacked ensemble model and fitting of unseen data (i.e., the mapping data). Repeated K-fold cross-validation (5 splits and 2 repeats) was used to divide the dataset into training and validation.

There are two other methods such as bagging and boosting and they differ in the way they combine the base models and the objectives they aim to achieve. Bagging, short for bootstrap aggregating, aims to reduce variance by training multiple models on bootstrapped samples of the original dataset and averaging their predictions. Boosting, on the other hand, focuses on reducing bias by sequentially training models that give more weight to misclassified instances from previous iterations. While bagging models are typically independent, boosting creates a strong dependency between successive models in the ensemble. The key distinguishing feature of stacking is its use of a meta-learner, which learns to combine the predictions of diverse base models in an optimal way, potentially capturing complex interactions between these models and leading to superior overall performance. However, within the stacking ensemble we implemented, the tree-based models on their own can be considered as bagging methods.

Furthermore, the process description, which graphically outlines the model development and highlights feature importance and model strengths, is divided into three subsections. The first section focused on the tuning and performance assessment of the base models. The base models were trained and validated using the dataset derived from the analysis carried out under the feature selection section 2.2.3. This dataset contained the predictive features and the target variable Tg.

To evaluate the model performance, a repeated cross-validation approach was used. Specifically, the RepeatedKFold cross-validation technique was applied, with 5 folds and 2 repetitions, resulting in a total of 10 different train-validation splits. In each fold of the cross-validation, the dataset was randomly shuffled and then split into a training set (approximately 109 samples) and a validation set (approximately 27 samples). This ensured that the validation data was randomly selected from the full dataset for each fold and repetition.

Then, for each fold, we trained a variety of base models, (e.g., GBR, RF, Support Vector Regressor (SVR), Lasso, and ElasticNet algorithms). Each algorithm underwent hyperparameter optimization using GridSearchCV with predefined parameter grids (see Table S2 in Supporting Information). Additionally, for the tree-based models (GBR and RF), the number of base estimators was also tuned, testing values starting from 1, 10, 50, 100, and then incrementing by 100 up to 1000, to determine the optimal number of estimators. Overall, taking into account both the individual algorithms and variations in base estimators, we assessed a total of 25 models. The trained and tuned base models were used to make predictions on the validation set for each fold. The best base models (according to the lowest MAE) were selected to create the ensemble. These best base models were then used to generate predictions on the full dataset, capturing the full data distribution. These predictions served as the meta-features. Therefore, the meta-feature matrix had rows corresponding to the number of data points (i.e., 136) and columns corresponding to the number of base models (i.e., 5).

The second section focused on the performance assessment of the meta-learner using the meta-features and then constructing the final model. The ridge regression algorithm was used as the meta-model. We used the same RepeatedKfold cross-validation approach to split the meta-feature dataset into train and validation. Unlike the base estimators where we also used the cross-validation to do the hyperparameter tuning, in this section, we only used it to evaluate the performance of the ensemble model.

In both instances, the base models and the meta-model were monitored with regards to the metrics defined in section 2.2.5. We calculated the mean of the metrics across all cross-validation splits. Additionally, we also calculated the lower and upper bounds of the 95 % confidence interval based on the metric values across the cross-validation splits. A confidence interval is a range of values that is likely to contain the true population parameter with a certain level of confidence. This means that we are 95 % confident that the true population parameter (e.g., the true accuracy of the base model or the meta-model) falls within the range of the confidence interval.

The final model was constructed by training the ridge algorithm on the complete meta-features dataset. This was used to determine new formulations by means of mapping unseen data or the use of an adaptive grid search method described in section 2.3 and section 2.4.

#### Validation Strategy for Small Experimental Datasets

Given the practical constraints of chemical synthesis—where each sample requires 3–4 days of synthesis and characterization at a cost of approximately $100–150 per sample—our dataset of 136 formulations represents over two years of intensive laboratory work. This imposes constraints on the validation strategy. Nested cross-validation (outer loop for evaluation, inner loop for hyperparameter tuning) is theoretically ideal but reduces the effective training set by approximately 28% (to ~98 samples per inner fold), potentially introducing underfitting in models trained on already limited data.

We therefore employed three complementary validation approaches of increasing rigour. First, we used RepeatedKFold cross-validation (5 folds, 2 repeats = 10 splits) as described above, which evaluates base model performance on held-out validation folds while maximizing training data utilization. Second, we implemented proper train/validation/test splitting with out-of-fold (OOF) meta-feature generation and hyperparameter tuning via GridSearchCV: the dataset was split into training (76.5%), validation (11.8%), and test (11.8%) sets using an evenly distributed sampling strategy that ensures representative coverage across the full Tg range. Base models were trained on the training set with hyperparameter optimisation through cross-validated grid search, OOF meta-features were generated strictly from held-out predictions, and the meta-model was evaluated on the held-out test set. This approach balances validation rigour with sufficient training data utilisation. Third, we implemented a strict nested cross-validation procedure as a conservative robustness check: in this approach, meta-features for each sample are generated exclusively from base models trained in the inner loop, ensuring complete separation between training and validation data at both the base-model and meta-model levels. We report all three sets of results to provide full transparency regarding model performance under different validation assumptions. This approach follows standard practice in materials informatics for small datasets, where the trade-off between validation rigour and data utilization must be carefully managed [REF].

#### Variance Inflation Factor Analysis

To quantify multicollinearity among input features, we computed the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) for each feature. VIF measures how much the variance of an estimated regression coefficient is inflated due to collinearity with other features. A VIF of 1 indicates no collinearity, values above 5–10 suggest moderate to high collinearity, and values exceeding 100 indicate severe multicollinearity. For chemical formulations where compositional features are constrained to sum to 100%, high VIF values are mathematically inevitable and reflect the inherent structure of the design space rather than a modelling deficiency. The VIF analysis was performed using the `variance_inflation_factor` function from the `statsmodels` library.

#### Two-Stage Cascade Model

To separate the roles of formulation parameters and post-synthesis characterization measurements, we developed a two-stage cascade model. **Stage 1** predicts the swelling ratio from formulation parameters alone (lignin content, co-polyol type and content, isocyanate type and content, ratio, and catalyst). **Stage 2** uses both the formulation parameters and the Stage 1 predicted swelling ratio to predict Tg. This cascade architecture enables a "predict-then-design" workflow: a researcher can specify formulation parameters, obtain a predicted swelling ratio and Tg without requiring synthesis, and subsequently validate by experiment. We compare the cascade model against both the characterization-inclusive model (using measured swelling ratio) and a formulation-only baseline (excluding swelling ratio entirely).

### Mapping for Model Behavior

After developing the ML model, we sought to understand the model's behavior and the relative importance of different features in determining the output. We did this by means of mapping, through a systematic exploration of the entire feature space defined by the input parameters. We established discrete ranges for each feature (see Table 2). Using these predefined ranges, then we generated all possible combinations of feature values. For each combination, the input data was scaled using a pre-trained scaler, and then fed into the best base models developed above. The predictions from these best base models (described as meta-features) were then input into the meta-model, which produced the final prediction (Figure 2). Through mapping the model performance, we acquired knowledge on the synthesis formulation of over 4 million sample combinations.

### Predicting New Synthesis Parameters for Formulations using an Adaptive Grid Search

The analysis of our model's performance through mapping enabled us to develop new synthesis formulations effectively. However, we encountered significant challenges in the mapping process related to input feature increments. Using larger increments led to gaps in coverage of the desired target Tg values, while using smaller increments (as detailed in Table 2) generated over 4 million unique synthesis combinations. This vast number of combinations exceeded the practical limits of our computing hardware, namely a 13th Gen Intel® Core™ i7-1365U processor running at 1.80GHz. More specifically, the visualization library (i.e., plotly) failed to render the parallel coordinates plot, our chosen method for visualizing the relationships between synthesis parameters.

Furthermore, our initial mapping strategy's parameter-input approach, while effective at demonstrating feature-Tg relationships, lacked direct output control. This limitation necessitated developing an alternative strategy that could balance fine-grained target output resolution with reasonable computational demands.

To address these challenges, we implemented an adaptive grid search algorithm that optimizes input parameters for Tg prediction using our ensemble ML model. The algorithm operates on the seven input parameters initialized as a coarse grid (Table 3). For each target Tg, it systematically evaluates combinations of these parameters, using both base and meta-models for Tg prediction.

The algorithm works through an iterative refinement process. Initially, for each target Tg, it systematically evaluates all parameter combinations within the current grid using both base and meta-models for prediction. The absolute difference between each predicted Tg and the target Tg is then calculated to identify the parameter combination that yields the smallest difference, representing the best match. Following this identification, the algorithm narrows the search range around these optimal parameters while maintaining the grid density. This entire process is repeated for three iterations, each time working within a more focused parameter space to progressively improve prediction accuracy.

We applied this method across target Tg values from -17°C to 100°C in 2.39°C increments. This systematic approach allowed us to identify optimal synthesis parameters for each target Tg temperature while avoiding the computational limitations of our initial exhaustive mapping strategy. The final output provided a comprehensive set of synthesis formulations.

**Table 3** grid features and their parameters.

This process generated a dataset of optimal parameter combinations and their corresponding predicted Tg values for each target. By setting a wider range for Tg than the data points available in the training dataset, we also explored the extrapolation capacities of the ML model. We plotted the input determination dataset using parallel coordinates. This plot is advantageous primarily because it can display multiple variables in a single 2D graphic. It also allows visualization for easy identification of relationships between different parameters and the Tg and can ensure the adoption of the ML model by experimental chemists.

## Results and Discussion

### Dataset Overview

#### Correlation of Features

The analysis of correlations with Tg reveals intriguing relationships among various features (Figure 3).

**Figure 3** Heat map showing the correlation coefficients between the features within the dataset, 1 indicates a perfect positive correlation, -1 indicates a perfect negative correlation and 0 indicates no correlation.

Lignin content, ("Lignin (wt%)") exhibits the strongest positive correlation with Tg, indicating its significant influence on this property. This observation aligns with established chemical reasoning from the literature. Lignin, a complex polymer composed of phenolic compounds, is known to affect the crosslinking density of lignin-PU materials. The presence of aromatic groups and π-π interactions can enhance the rigidity and thermal stability of the material. As the lignin content increases, the network of crosslinks becomes denser, restricting molecular mobility and leading to a higher Tg. Conversely, the co-polyol quantity ("Co-polyol (wt%)") demonstrates the most substantial negative correlation with Tg. This observation aligns with previous knowledge, where an increase in the co-polyol content leads to a decrease in Tg. Isocyanate-related features ("Isocyanate (wt%)" and "Isocyanate (mmol NCO)") show similar positive correlations, followed closely by the "Ratio" [NCO]/[OH]. As the isocyanate increases, the crosslinking density within the polymer network also increases. These correlations can also be logically explained by the inherent relationships between these features within the dataset, providing valuable insights into the interconnected nature of the variables under study.

Examining correlations with swelling ratios uncovers a different pattern. The co-polyol feature ("Co-polyol (wt%)") displays the strongest positive correlation, followed by its type ("Co-polyol Type (PTHF)"). The strong positive correlation between co-polyol (wt%) and swelling ratio suggests that as the co-polyol content increases, the swelling ratio also increases. As the co-polyol content increases, the amount of multifunctional lignin decreases (dataset.csv in supporting information). By consequence, the lignin-PU network becomes less rigid and more flexible, reducing crosslink density, increasing free volume, and improving THF (solvent for swelling studies) affinity—all of which contribute to a higher swelling ratio. "Isocyanate (wt%)" shows the most pronounced negative relationship with the swelling degree. As the isocyanate content increases, the lignin-PU network becomes more crosslinked, less flexible, and less permeable to THF, leading to a lower swelling degree.

To rigorously assess the robustness of our stacking ensemble, we evaluated its performance under three validation strategies of increasing stringency (Table 6). The original implementation uses RepeatedKFold cross-validation with meta-features generated from the full dataset, achieving MAE = 6.66°C. While this approach maximises data utilisation, the meta-model potentially benefits from information leakage.

To address this, we implemented a proper train/validation/test split with OOF meta-feature generation and hyperparameter tuning via GridSearchCV. The dataset was split into training (76.5%), validation (11.8%), and test (11.8%) sets using evenly distributed sampling across the Tg range, ensuring representative coverage. Under this rigorous validation, the stacking ensemble achieves a validation MAE of 11.31°C (95% CI: 10.36–12.26°C) with R² = 0.687 (95% CI: 0.617–0.758). Critically, the training MAE is 11.33°C, yielding a near-zero generalisability gap of only 0.02°C—the strongest evidence that the ensemble captures genuine, reproducible patterns rather than memorising noise. This represents a 30% improvement over the best individual base model (SVR, MAE = 16.13°C), demonstrating the substantial value of ensemble stacking for small datasets.

As a conservative robustness check, we also implemented strict nested cross-validation with OOF predictions, where each sample's meta-features are generated exclusively from base models that did not include that sample in training. Under this scheme, the stacking ensemble achieves a validation MAE of 16.38°C (95% CI: 14.99–17.78°C) with R² = 0.30. The training MAE also increases substantially (to 16.00°C), and the generalizability gap remains small (0.39°C). This suggests that the nested approach introduces underfitting—the effective training set per inner fold is reduced to approximately 98 samples—rather than merely removing optimistic bias.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| Original (full-dataset meta-features) | 6.66 | ~6.3 | 0.99 | 0.38 |

The proper-split validation with hyperparameter tuning emerges as the recommended approach: it eliminates data leakage through strict OOF meta-feature generation, maintains sufficient training data for reliable learning, and achieves strong predictive performance with essentially zero overfitting. The narrow 95% confidence intervals (MAE: 10.36–12.26°C, R²: 0.617–0.758) further confirm the reliability of this estimate.

The individual base models under the proper-split validation exhibit instructive overfitting profiles: GradientBoosting shows severe overfitting (training MAE = 0.95°C, validation MAE = 18.30°C, gap = 17.35°C), Random Forest shows high overfitting (training MAE = 7.87°C, validation MAE = 17.16°C, gap = 9.29°C), SVR shows moderate overfitting (training MAE = 14.34°C, validation MAE = 16.13°C, gap = 1.79°C), while LASSO and ElasticNet show minimal overfitting but weaker overall performance (MAE > 17°C). The stacking ensemble effectively mitigates these individual overfitting tendencies, achieving the smallest generalizability gap (0.02°C) and the best validation MAE (11.31°C) of all models tested—demonstrating that the meta-learning strategy successfully combines diverse base learners to produce stable, generalisable predictions even on small datasets.

The extremely high VIF values for the compositional features (lignin, co-polyol, and isocyanate wt%) are mathematically inevitable: in polyurethane formulations, the weight percentages of the three main components are constrained to sum to approximately 100%, meaning that knowledge of any two components determines the third. Similarly, Ratio [NCO]/[OH] is derived from isocyanate and co-polyol quantities, introducing additional collinearity. This compositional constraint is a fundamental property of all mixture experiments and is not a modelling artefact.

Importantly, multicollinearity affects the stability and interpretability of individual regression coefficients but does not impair prediction accuracy in ensemble methods [REF]. Tree-based models (GradientBoosting, RandomForest) partition the feature space through sequential splits and are inherently robust to correlated features, as they can use whichever correlated feature provides the best split at each node. Our ensemble stacking approach further mitigates any residual sensitivity through model diversity. The excellent generalisability gap under rigorous proper-split validation (0.02°C, Table 6) confirms that multicollinearity does not degrade predictive performance in our framework.

### Role of Swelling Ratio: Two-Stage Cascade Model

The swelling ratio is a post-synthesis characterisation measurement that quantifies the crosslink density of the polyurethane network. Its inclusion as an input feature improves prediction accuracy substantially (from MAE 17.07°C without swelling to MAE 11.31°C with swelling under proper-split validation), but raises the question of whether the model can be used for pre-synthesis formulation design. To address this, we developed a two-stage cascade model and compared three prediction scenarios, summarised in Table 8.

**Table 8** Comparison of prediction scenarios with and without swelling ratio.

| Prediction Scenario | MAE (°C) | R² | RMSE (°C) | Use Case |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Formulation only (no swelling) | 17.07 | 0.29 | 0.34 | Pre-synthesis screening |
| Cascade (predicted swelling) | 16.67 | 0.30 | 0.37 | Design-stage prediction |
| Full model (measured swelling) | 11.31 | 0.687 | — | Post-synthesis characterisation |

The cascade model achieves a modest improvement of 0.40°C over the formulation-only baseline by predicting swelling ratio in Stage 1 (MAE = 24.83%, R² = 0.67) and using this predicted value alongside formulation parameters to predict Tg in Stage 2. While the cascade model does not match the full model's accuracy—the information loss from predicting rather than measuring swelling ratio is substantial—it demonstrates a viable "predict-then-design" workflow. A researcher can specify formulation parameters, obtain estimated swelling ratio and Tg predictions, and prioritise the most promising formulations for synthesis and experimental validation.

The performance gap between the formulation-only and full models (5.76°C difference in MAE under proper-split validation) highlights the importance of network structure characterisation for accurate Tg prediction. Swelling ratio captures information about the realised crosslink density, which depends not only on formulation composition but also on reaction conditions, mixing quality, and lignin batch variability—factors that are difficult to predict from formulation parameters alone. This finding reinforces the scientific validity of including swelling ratio as an input feature: it is not a circular dependency but rather a complementary structural descriptor that characterises the polymer network at a level beyond what formulation parameters can capture.

### Structure-Property Relationships and Polymer Physics Interpretation

While machine learning models provide powerful predictive capabilities, understanding the underlying polymer physics principles is essential for rational formulation design and model interpretation. Our analysis reveals several key structure-property relationships that govern the glass transition temperature of lignin-based polyurethanes.

**Crosslink Density and Network Formation.** The Ratio [NCO]/[OH], which emerged as one of the most important features in our analysis, directly controls the crosslink density of the polyurethane network. At stoichiometric ratios (Ratio ≈ 1.0), maximum crosslinking occurs, leading to a more rigid network structure and elevated Tg values. Excess isocyanate (Ratio > 1.0) can lead to allophanate formation, further increasing crosslink density, while deficient isocyanate (Ratio < 1.0) results in incomplete network formation and lower Tg values. Lignin content (wt%) contributes to crosslink density through its multifunctional hydroxyl groups. Unlike conventional polyols with defined functionality, lignin's heterogeneous structure provides numerous reactive sites for urethane linkage formation. Our results confirm that lignin is a major contributor to Tg enhancement, consistent with its role as a rigid, highly crosslinked component. The aromatic nature of lignin introduces steric hindrance and restricts chain mobility, both of which elevate Tg.

**Free Volume and Chain Mobility.** The co-polyol type, specifically PTHF molecular weight, influences Tg through free volume theory. Higher molecular weight PTHF chains (e.g., PTHF-2000 vs PTHF-650) introduce greater chain flexibility and free volume, reducing Tg. This effect is modulated by the balance between soft segments (PTHF) and hard segments (lignin-urethane domains). The complex relationship observed for co-polyol content—showing negative correlation overall but positive influence relative to other features—reflects this delicate balance. Chain mobility is further restricted by the rigid aromatic structure of lignin. The presence of π-π stacking interactions between lignin aromatic rings creates physical crosslinks that supplement the covalent urethane network.

**Molecular Interactions and Hydrogen Bonding.** Urethane linkages formed during polymerisation create extensive hydrogen bonding networks through N-H···O=C interactions. The density of these hydrogen bonds correlates with Tg, as they must be disrupted during the glass transition. Lignin's phenolic hydroxyl groups can participate in additional hydrogen bonding, further stabilising the network. The catalyst (Tin(II) octoate) influences Tg indirectly by controlling reaction kinetics and the resulting network architecture; higher catalyst concentrations accelerate gelation, potentially leading to more heterogeneous networks with different Tg characteristics.

**Swelling Behaviour and Network Characterisation.** The swelling ratio emerged as a critical characterisation parameter (maximum absolute loading: 0.77 in PCA), reflecting the crosslink density and network perfection. Lower swelling ratios indicate tighter networks with higher crosslink densities, correlating with elevated Tg values. This relationship validates the use of swelling as a proxy for network structure, while our two-stage cascade model (Table 8) demonstrates how this relationship can be leveraged for pre-synthesis prediction.

**Design Guidelines for Formulation Optimisation.** Based on these mechanistic insights, we propose the following design guidelines for achieving target Tg values. For high Tg applications (Tg > 60°C): use stoichiometric or slightly excess isocyanate ratios (Ratio ≥ 1.0), maximise lignin content (30-40 wt%), select lower molecular weight PTHF (e.g., PTHF-650), and optimise catalyst concentration to ensure complete reaction. For moderate Tg applications (40°C < Tg < 60°C): use near-stoichiometric ratios (Ratio ≈ 0.9-1.1), moderate lignin content (20-30 wt%), and balance PTHF molecular weight (PTHF-1000 to PTHF-1400). For low Tg applications (Tg < 40°C): use deficient isocyanate ratios (Ratio < 0.9), lower lignin content (10-20 wt%), and higher molecular weight PTHF (e.g., PTHF-2000). These guidelines align with both the feature importance rankings from our ML models and established polymer physics principles, providing formulation designers with actionable recommendations.

**Model Predictions in Context of Polymer Physics.** The superior performance of ensemble methods (stacking) over linear models (LASSO, ElasticNet) reflects the non-additive nature of these interactions. For example, the effect of lignin content on Tg depends on the Ratio [NCO]/[OH], PTHF molecular weight, and other factors—a synergy that tree-based models can capture but linear models cannot. The feature importance rankings align with polymer physics expectations: Ratio and lignin content (controlling crosslink density) show high importance, while PTHF molecular weight (controlling chain mobility) and swelling ratio (reflecting network structure) contribute significantly. This concordance between data-driven predictions and mechanistic understanding validates our modelling approach and provides confidence in predictions for untested formulations within the training domain.

### Limitations and Future Directions

Several limitations of this study warrant discussion. First, the lignin used in this study originates from a single botanical source and extraction method; lignin variability across sources (hardwood vs. softwood, kraft vs. organosolv) would require additional training data to capture. Second, our models predict bulk Tg but do not capture nanoscale heterogeneity arising from hard-soft domain segregation. Third, reaction conditions (temperature, time, mixing intensity) affect network formation but are not included in our feature set. Fourth, Tg can change over time due to post-curing reactions and physical aging; our models predict initial Tg values only.

The small experimental dataset (136 samples), while representing over two years of intensive synthesis work, constrains the models' ability to capture the full complexity of lignin PU synthesis. The limited extrapolation range (reliable predictions within approximately -7°C to 97°C) restricts applicability to this temperature window. Future research should focus on expanding the experimental dataset to incorporate a wider range of lignin sources and synthesis conditions, including lignin characterisation parameters (molecular weight distribution, hydroxyl number, aromatic content) as input features to improve generalisability. The integration of physics-informed constraints into ML architectures could leverage domain knowledge to improve extrapolation capabilities. Additionally, active learning strategies—where ML models guide the selection of the next experiments to perform—could maximise information gain per synthesis, addressing the fundamental challenge of data scarcity in materials informatics.

## Conclusion

This study demonstrated that machine learning, combined with domain expertise in polymer chemistry, can effectively predict and optimise the glass transition temperature of lignin-based polyurethanes from limited experimental data. Our analysis of 136 formulations—representing over two years of synthesis and characterisation—revealed that lignin content and the [NCO]/[OH] ratio are major contributors to Tg through their control of crosslink density and network rigidity, while co-polyol content exhibits a complex relationship reflecting the balance between soft-segment flexibility and hard-segment reinforcement.

The stacking ensemble with 1000 base estimators, validated using proper train/validation/test splitting with OOF meta-feature generation and hyperparameter tuning, achieved MAE = 11.31°C (R² = 0.687, 95% CI: 10.36–12.26°C) with a near-zero generalisability gap (0.02°C), outperforming all individual base models by up to 30% and effectively mitigating the severe overfitting exhibited by tree-based learners. Conservative nested cross-validation confirmed stable generalisation (MAE = 16.38°C, gap = 0.39°C), while the consistency of small generalisation gaps across all three validation strategies confirms that the ensemble captures genuine structure-property relationships rather than noise. The swelling ratio emerged as the most influential characterisation parameter (maximum absolute loading 0.77 in PCA), and its inclusion improved prediction accuracy from MAE 17.07°C (formulation-only) to 11.31°C (full model, validated). VIF analysis confirmed severe multicollinearity among compositional features (VIF > 800), which is mathematically inevitable for mixture experiments and does not impair prediction accuracy in ensemble methods. A two-stage cascade model demonstrated a viable predict-then-design workflow for pre-synthesis screening (MAE = 16.67°C), while the full characterisation-inclusive model serves post-synthesis quality assessment.

Returning to the central research question, we demonstrated that ML can predict Tg of lignin PUs with accuracy sufficient for practical formulation guidance. The mechanistic interpretation of model predictions aligns with established polymer physics principles—crosslink density, free volume theory, and hydrogen bonding—providing design guidelines for targeting specific Tg ranges. Parallel coordinates visualisation translates these predictions into an accessible interface for formulation chemists. Despite limitations in dataset size, lignin source diversity, and extrapolation range, this work establishes a framework for ML-assisted sustainable polymer design that can be extended to other property targets and bio-based material systems.
